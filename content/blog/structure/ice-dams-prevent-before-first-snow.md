Snow season is close. According to The Weather Channel's first-snow outlook (October 1), the northern tier of the Midwest typically sees its first accumulating snow in October and much of the Great Lakes region in November, and this winter's strong El Niño could shift those dates around the country.

That makes late fall the best time to deal with **ice dams**: the ridges of ice that build up at the edge of a roof and push meltwater back under the shingles. Once the roof is covered in snow, your options shrink to a roof rake and hoping for the best. Right now you can still get into the attic, and that's where the real fix is.

## What causes an ice dam

An ice dam needs three things: snow on the roof, heat to melt it, and cold to refreeze the water. Michigan State University Extension describes the pattern: heat from the attic warms the roof and melts the snow, the water runs down to the eaves (the coldest part of the roof, because they hang out past the heated house), and it freezes again. The ice grows, water pools behind it, and that water can back up under the shingles and leak into the attic, walls and ceilings.

It doesn't take a blizzard. UMass Amherst's Building and Construction Technology program notes that ice dams can form with only **1 to 2 inches of snow** on a poorly insulated, poorly air-sealed roof if several days below freezing follow.

The damage often shows up late. Wet insulation compresses and loses R-value, and water in wall cavities can lead to rot, mold and peeling paint long after the ice is gone. NDSU Extension also lists ceiling stains and structural damage.

## The fix is in the attic, not on the roof

Every extension source we checked says the same thing: keep the roof deck cold by keeping house heat out of the attic. As UMass Amherst's Paul Fisette put it: "Whatever plan you decide to follow, focus on the cause."

That comes down to three jobs.

### 1. Seal air leaks first

Warm air leaking into the attic carries far more heat than you'd think. NDSU Extension points to openings where plumbing, heating and electrical lines pass through the ceiling, recessed "can" lights, and attic hatches or pull-down ladders. UMass adds chimneys, bathroom exhaust fans and the gaps where interior walls meet the ceiling. Seal them with caulk, spray foam or weatherstripping before you add any insulation, because insulation on top makes them hard to reach later.

### 2. Add insulation

NDSU Extension says attics in northern climates need roughly **15 inches of blown-in insulation, or about R-50**. ENERGY STAR's recommendation is similar: if you already have 3 to 4 inches, bring the attic up to **R-49** in climate zones 4 through 8 (or R-60 for an uninsulated attic).

**Example:** a 1,500 sq ft attic with 4 inches of old loose fiberglass. Using ENERGY STAR's figure of about R-2.5 per inch, that's roughly R-10 today, so you need to add about **R-39** to reach R-49. ### 3. Keep the soffit vents open

Cold outside air should flow in at the soffits and out at the ridge or gable vents, carrying off any heat that does get into the attic. Michigan State warns that insulation pushed out over the eaves blocks those vents and keeps the roof edge too warm. NDSU recommends baffles to hold insulation back from the soffits, and UMass suggests leaving about a 2-inch air channel between the insulation and the roof deck.

## A backup layer at the eaves

If you're reroofing this fall, ask about the **ice barrier**, a self-adhering membrane (often called ice and water shield) that goes under the shingles along the eaves. The International Residential Code requires one in areas with a history of ice dams, extending from the roof edge to **at least 24 inches inside the exterior wall line** of the house. Your local code may ask for more.

UMass calls the membrane a "band-aid": useful insurance if a dam forms, but not a cure for a warm attic.

> **Fun fact:** That 24-inch rule is measured level, from the inside of the wall, so the membrane has to run much farther up the slope than you'd guess. With a 12-inch overhang and a 6-inch-thick wall, the horizontal distance is 42 inches. On a 6/12 roof that's about **47 inches** along the slope, on an 8/12 roof about **50.5 inches**, and on a 12/12 roof about **59.4 inches**. A standard roll is only 36 inches wide, so most roofs need two courses. Our [roofing calculator](/roofing-calculator/) shows your roof's pitch factor, squares and shingle bundles.

**Example:** a 50 × 30 ft gable house with 12-inch overhangs has two eaves of about 52 ft each, or 104 ft of roof edge. Two courses means about 208 ft of membrane, or **3 rolls** if they're 75 ft long (the size UMass cites), before end laps. Follow the manufacturer's lap instructions and round up.

## What not to do

- **Don't count on heat cables.** NDSU says homeowners often use heat tape to treat the symptom instead of preventing the problem. UMass's article goes further, listing wasted energy, fire risk and leaks at the fasteners.
- **Don't chip ice with hammers, shovels or picks.** UMass warns these do more damage to the roof (and to you) than the ice does.
- **Don't climb on a snowy roof.** If you need to clear snow, NDSU recommends a roof rake from the ground, clearing the lower part of the roof where ice dams usually form, and keeping the rake away from overhead power lines. Go gently: UMass notes raking can scrape granules off asphalt shingles.
- **Don't just patch a leak.** After an ice dam leak, check the insulation, roof deck and walls for hidden water damage.

## A quick fall checklist

1. On a cold day, look in the attic for frost, stained decking or matted insulation.
2. Seal the gaps around pipes, wires, lights, the hatch and the chimney.
3. Measure the depth of your insulation and top it up to R-49 or more.
4. Make sure the soffit vents aren't buried.
5. If you're reroofing, confirm the quote includes ice barrier to at least 24 inches inside the wall line.
