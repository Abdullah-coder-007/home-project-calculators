It's peak leaf season, and a familiar slogan is back in the news: **"Leave the leaves."** In Fort Wayne, Indiana, the Eco Fest group is selling yard signs that encourage neighbors to skip the rake. In western North Carolina, WLOS ran a story on October 23 about how leaf piles shelter caterpillars and firefly larvae over the winter. And a UC Master Gardener column in California's Mid Valley Times made the same pitch on October 22.

The idea isn't just for wildlife. Fallen leaves are free organic matter, and the same leaves you'd bag for the curb can feed your lawn and mulch your garden beds. Here's how to do it without smothering your grass, based on what university extension services recommend.

## Why leaves are worth keeping

Leaves break down into organic matter that helps soil hold moisture and slowly returns nutrients. Penn State Extension notes that leaf mold (fully broken-down leaves) "is not high in nutrients," so think of leaves as a soil builder and mulch, not a fertilizer.

There's also the habitat angle that's driving this year's campaigns. Many insects overwinter in leaf litter, and birds depend on those insects in spring. As the Xerces Society's Scott Hoffman Black put it, quoted in a Mid Valley Times gardening column: "Many species rely on fallen leaves for cover and to insulate them from the elements."

You don't have to turn your whole yard into a forest floor. A common middle ground: mow the leaves on the lawn, move extras into beds, and leave one out-of-the-way corner alone.

## On the lawn: mow them, don't bury the grass

Whole leaves matted on the grass block light. Shredded leaves sift down between the blades and disappear. University research backs this up:

- **Michigan State University** turf researchers studied lawns that had ground-up leaves left in place and found the lawns did better with a layer of mulched leaves. Colorado State University Extension adds that MSU saw **fewer dandelions and crabgrass after three years** of leaf mulching.
- **Purdue** research, also cited by Colorado State, found that mulching leaves into lawns at high rates **didn't affect turf quality, color or soil pH.**

How to do it, per MSU and Colorado State Extension:

1. **Raise the mower to its highest setting** and remove the bagger.
2. **Make a pass or two.** Colorado State suggests at least two passes. MSU says a mulching mower can handle **up to 6 inches of leaves in one pass**; a regular mower may need more.
3. **Check your work.** Colorado State says the layer is thin enough if you can see grass through it. Penn State puts it at **up to about half an inch** of shredded leaves.
4. **Repeat as needed.** During heavy leaf drop, MSU notes you may be doing this twice in one week.

If you seeded or overseeded this fall, keep new grass especially clear. (We covered that in our [October grass seed guide](/blog/outdoor/is-october-too-late-to-plant-grass-seed/).)

## In garden beds: shred first, then spread

Leaves that won't fit on the lawn make good mulch for perennial beds, shrubs and trees. Shredding matters here too: Colorado State Extension says shredded leaves are preferred, and shredding helps them break down faster.

How deep to go depends on the source:

| Where | Depth | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lawn (mowed in) | Up to about ½ in | Penn State Extension |
| Around trees, shrubs, perennial beds | 2 to 4 in | Penn State Extension |
| Beds with sandy or loamy soil | Up to 5 in (settles to 3 to 4 in) | Alabama Extension |
| Beds with clay soil | About 2 to 3 in | Alabama Extension |

Alabama Extension says 3 to 4 inches of settled leaves is the maximum before the soil can run short of oxygen, so don't keep piling on. A good default for most beds is **about 3 inches** of shredded leaves.

An easy way to shred them: run the mower over a pile with the bagger on, then empty the bag into your beds.

## How many leaves does a bed need?

Here's where people get surprised. Mulch is measured in cubic feet or cubic yards, and the math is the same whether you buy bark mulch or use your own leaves.

**Example: a 4 × 25 ft bed along a fence (100 sq ft), 3 inches deep.**

- Volume: 100 sq ft × (3 ÷ 12) ft = **25 cubic feet**, or about **0.93 cubic yard**.
- If you bought bagged mulch instead, that's **13 bags** of 2 cu ft each.

**Example: an 8 ft circle around a tree, 3 inches deep.**

- Area: about 50 sq ft. Volume: about **12.6 cubic feet** (just under half a cubic yard), or **7 bags** of 2 cu ft.

These numbers come from our [mulch calculator](/mulch-calculator/), which uses area × depth and rounds bags up. Assumptions: settled depth of 3 inches, 2 cu ft bags, no extra for waste.

> **Fun fact:** Shredding can shrink leaves to as little as one-tenth of their original volume, according to Alabama Extension. So the 25 cubic feet of shredded leaves for that 100 sq ft bed could start out as roughly 250 cubic feet of loose leaves, a pile more than 9 cubic yards. Measure your beds with the [mulch calculator](/mulch-calculator/) first, so you know how many leaves to save before the rest go to the lawn or the compost pile.

If your beds are oddly shaped, the [square footage calculator](/square-footage-calculator/) adds up rectangles, triangles and circles, and the [cubic yard calculator](/cubic-yard-calculator/) turns that area and depth into yards.

## What to do with the extras

Most yards with big trees produce more leaves than the lawn and beds can use. Some options:

- **Compost them.** Leaves are high in carbon ("browns"), so Colorado State Extension recommends mixing them with nitrogen-rich "greens" such as grass clippings or vegetable scraps. Alabama Extension says whole leaves alone take about **two years** to break down; shredding and turning the pile speeds that up.
- **Make leaf mold.** Penn State suggests a simple wire bin **3 to 6 feet across and at least 3 feet tall.** Shredding, turning and watering can cut the time from years to **6 to 12 months.**
- **Add them to the vegetable garden.** Colorado State says you can add 6 to 8 inches of leaves to a vegetable or annual flower garden each year. Shredded leaves are better, and a soil test helps you avoid over-amending.
- **Leave a wild corner.** A patch under shrubs or along a back fence gives insects their winter cover.

## The bottom line

You don't need to bag every leaf. Mow what's on the lawn until you can see grass through it, shred the extras into beds at about 3 inches deep, and compost the rest. Measure your beds before you start so you know how much to keep.
