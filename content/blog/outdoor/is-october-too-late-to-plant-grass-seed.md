Every October, the same question takes over lawn searches. In Google Trends this month, **"is october too late to plant grass seed"** became a breakout search in the US, and **"is it too late to plant grass seed"** jumped about 600%. Close behind: "how long for grass seed to sprout" and "winter rye grass seed."

The short answer: **for most northern lawns, mid-October is late but not hopeless,** if you change how you do it. Here's what university turf specialists recommend.

## First, which grass do you have?

- **Cool-season grasses** (tall fescue, Kentucky bluegrass, perennial ryegrass, fine fescues) grow in the northern half of the US and the transition zone. Fall is their best planting season.
- **Warm-season grasses** (Bermuda, zoysia, St. Augustine, centipede) grow across the South. They are seeded or sodded in late spring and early summer, when the soil is warm. October seeding won't establish them.

Everything below is about cool-season lawns.

## The ideal window has mostly passed

UMass Amherst's turf program puts the best seeding window in Massachusetts at roughly **the third week of August to the third week of September**. Further south it runs a little later, and further north a little earlier. Each week past that window, seedlings go into winter smaller and thinner.

UMass's advice for this time of year:

- **Avoid starting a brand-new lawn from bare, tilled soil after the end of September** if you can wait.
- **Overseeding an existing lawn and patching small bare spots can continue into October,** though results fall off as the weeks go by.
- **Pick fast growers.** Perennial ryegrass establishes quickly, which gives it the most room for late planting. Kentucky bluegrass is among the slowest.

## Plan B: dormant seeding

If you've missed the window, you can seed *so late* that nothing sprouts until spring. This is called dormant seeding:

- **When:** once the soil has dropped into the low 40s°F and air temperatures stay below 50°F, but before the ground freezes. Too late is better than too early, because seed that sprouts in a warm spell can be killed by the next hard freeze.
- **How:** get the seed into contact with soil (slit-seed, or rake it into bare spots). Seeding just before a forecast snow can help settle it.
- **How much:** use a **higher rate** than normal, because fewer seeds survive the winter.
- **Best for:** thickening thin turf and filling bare patches, rather than starting a whole new lawn. UMass notes that it suits Kentucky bluegrass better than perennial ryegrass.

## Don't smother it with leaves

Leaves are the other October job, and they matter for new grass. University of Minnesota Extension recommends **mowing leaves into the lawn** instead of raking them: run the mower over dry leaves, twice if needed, until the pieces are small. It returns nutrients and helps keep weed seeds from sprouting.

The limit: once **less than half of the grass is visible** under the leaves, bag the extra or compost it. A thick leaf layer blocks light and can encourage snow mold over winter, and new seedlings are the first to suffer.

> **Fun fact:** One of the fastest-rising lawn searches this month is "do squirrels eat grass seed" (+170%). Birds and squirrels do pick at seed left sitting on the surface, which is one more reason to rake it in. If you're dormant seeding at a higher rate, start with the rate on your seed bag and nudge it up. ## Your October lawn checklist

1. **Measure the area you're seeding.** A 25 × 40 ft patch is 1,000 sq ft, which needs 6 lb of seed at a typical new-lawn tall fescue rate of 6 lb per 1,000 sq ft (always check your bag).
2. **Mow leaves into the lawn** while you can still see at least half the grass.
3. **Keep new seed moist** with light, frequent watering until it's established.
4. **Mulching beds for winter?** The [mulch calculator](/mulch-calculator/) gives you bags or cubic yards for a 2–3 in layer.
