How the gravel driveway calculator works

A gravel driveway that lasts is built in layers: big stone at the bottom to carry the weight, a middle layer that locks into it, and a surface of smaller stone with fines that packs hard. The calculator works out each layer's volume from your area and its finished depth, turns it into tons and rounds up to the half tons that quarries load.

Volume of a layer (cu yd) = area (sq ft) × depth (in) ÷ 12 ÷ 27
Clean stone (#3, #57) = cubic yards × 1.4 tons
Surface stone with fines (#411, crusher run) = cubic yards × 2.16 tons per compacted cubic yard
Tons to order = tons × (1 + extra %), rounded up to the next half ton
Fabric = area × 1.1

The 2.16 figure comes from Penn State's Center for Dirt and Gravel Roads, which estimates 0.04 tons of surface aggregate per square foot for a layer 6 inches deep after compaction (placed 8 inches loose). Clean stone has no fines to fill its gaps, so it settles much less; the 10% extra covers spreading and soft spots.

Worked example

A driveway 60 ft long and 12 ft wide is 720 sq ft. Each 4 inch layer is 720 × 4 ÷ 12 ÷ 27 = 8.89 cubic yards.

  • Base, #3 stone: 8.89 × 1.4 × 1.1 = 13.7 tons, so order 14 tons.
  • Middle, #57 stone: the same, 14 tons.
  • Surface, #411: 8.89 × 2.16 × 1.1 = 21.1 tons, so order 21.5 tons.

That is 49.5 tons for 12 inches of stone, about four tandem truckloads, plus 792 sq ft of fabric.

Before you order

  • Strip grass and topsoil, and dig down so the finished surface sits a little above the ground around it.
  • On clay or wet soil, lay woven geotextile fabric first, overlapping seams by about a foot.
  • Compact each layer with a plate compactor or roller before the next goes on.
  • Shape the surface with a crown or cross slope. Penn State recommends 1/2 to 3/4 inch per foot, and Kansas LTAP about 4% (1/2 inch per foot).
  • Check the truck's weight against your culvert, septic lines and any lawn it must cross.
